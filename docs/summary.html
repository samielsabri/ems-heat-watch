Why

The health impact of heat is usually measured with hospital and mortality data that arrive months later. Ambulance dispatch data exist in near real time in every paramedic service, but are rarely used for heat surveillance.

What I did

  • Data: daily counts of NYC ambulance calls coded heat (about 800 a year), 2010 to September 2026, and daily maximum temperature at Central Park.
  • Indicator: each day, compare heat calls with the number expected on a normal-temperature day at that time of year. Alarm when calls are above the expected range.
  • Test: replay 2016–2026 day by day, using only past data, and check every heat wave (3+ days at 32 °C or more). Compare with two standard outbreak-detection methods. Nothing was tuned on 2025–26.

What it means for Toronto

  • Environment and Climate Change Canada warns 18–24 hours ahead for heat events of two or more days. An EMS indicator adds the health side: is the heat causing harm, and how much? It does not predict heat; it confirms impact the same day.
  • Single hot days usually get no warning. In NYC the indicator flagged 49% of them (2016–24).
  • Next step: the same indicator on Toronto Paramedic Services data, using humidex.

Limits

  • Dispatch codes record what callers and crews report, not diagnoses.
  • Only three heat waves in the held-out period. NYC data and one weather station; not yet tested in Toronto.
