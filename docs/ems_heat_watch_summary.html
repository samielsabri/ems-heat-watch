EMS Heat Watch

Heat-health surveillance from ambulance dispatch data: proof of concept on New York City EMS data, 2013–2026 · Sami El Sabri · code and results: github.com/samielsabri/ems-heat-watch · November 2026

Problem

Hospital and mortality data, the core measures of heat-health impact, arrive months after a heat event. Ambulance dispatch records exist in near real time inside every paramedic service, but are rarely integrated into heat surveillance. This project tests whether dispatch data alone can show the health impact of heat within days, using public NYC data.

Data

  • NYC EMS Incident Dispatch Data: 20,621,648 calls, 2013-01-01 to 2026-09-30; daily counts by call type, match the portal on every day. Public release runs ~6 weeks behind; an internal feed would be same-day.
  • NYC has a dedicated HEAT call type (~799/yr, 2016–24; 1,483 in 2026 to Sep). Pre-specified heat syndrome: unconscious, altered mental status, sick, breathing difficulty, cardiac, respiratory, arrest (1,561 calls/day).
  • Exposure: daily Tmax, Central Park (NOAA GHCN-D).

Method

  • Exposure–response: quasi-Poisson time series (time spline, day of week, holidays, threshold heat term, lags 0–3), 2013–2024. HEAT calls: +48% per °C above 20.5 °C (46–50%), almost all same-day. Syndrome: +1.0% per °C, no clear threshold.
  • Surveillance: weekly refit on the previous 3 years; alarm when calls exceed the 99.5% negative-binomial limit of the count expected at the normal Tmax for that date.
  • Comparators: EARS C1–C3 and Farrington Flexible (daily), ported from my earlier outbreak-detection project.
  • Back-test against heat waves (≥3 days Tmax ≥32.2 °C). Methods fixed on data to 2024; 2025–26 held out.

Findings

  • HEAT calls are a specific signal: the heat model alarmed on 19/20 development and 3/3 held-out heat waves with ~2 false alarms per season; Farrington had none but missed 4/20; EARS detected similarly but with 3–8× more false alarms.
  • Alarms come on day 0 because heat waves are defined from temperature: the value is same-day confirmation and measurement of health impact, not earlier warning than a forecast.
  • The broad syndrome rises only ~3–9% in a heat wave, inside its day-to-day noise: use it to estimate excess calls, not to alarm.

Fit with Toronto's heat response

ECCC issues Toronto Heat Warnings (≥2 days Tmax ≥31 °C with Tmin ≥20 °C, or humidex ≥40), graded by expected impact (Moderate/High/Extreme, "increased" to "extreme risk of illness or death"); TPH runs the Heat Relief Strategy. EMS excess calls measure that health impact directly: during a warning they confirm and size it the same day; after the event they test whether the impact level matched the harm. One-day heat events usually get no warning, yet the HEAT alarm fired on 49% of isolated hot days (2016–24).

Limitations

  • Dispatch codes are caller/crew impressions, not diagnoses; NYC re-labelled codes during the period (families merged).
  • Only 3 held-out heat waves; development results partly in-sample. Heat waves defined from temperature only.
  • Single station, Tmax only (no humidex yet); threshold model rather than DLNM; alarm limits ignore parameter uncertainty; Farrington port not yet cross-checked against R surveillance.
  • Not yet tested on Toronto data.
